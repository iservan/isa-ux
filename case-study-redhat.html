03 · The problem

41% of features in the high-tier cloud subscription were never set up.

Red Hat enterprise clients were buying cloud solutions they could not fully use. Teams had no shared view of technical skill, and unused product was treated as normal.

41% of features in the high-tier cloud subscription were never set up, the product was paid for and still invisible in daily work.
32% boost in cloud server utilization was possible with the right training.

These numbers came from observing at how enterprise clients had set up their high-tier subscriptions and checking it against survey results. The 32% is the gain in usage that training could still unlock. Together, those two figures defined the problem. My job after that was to make it clear to enterprise tech managers (B2B users) that training could help them get more value from their cloud solutions and make it more engaging for learners (B2C users).

Curriculum team had already documented business requirements in a long spreadsheet. Those requirements described what the business wanted to ship, not what users needed to.

04 · Research & discovery

I treated the business requirements as assumptions and I talked to the people involved in the process.

My first job was to validate the business requirements spreadsheet against user needs. I led the UX research, collaborating cross-functionally with Sales, Marketing, and Curriculum teams. The real challenge wasn't just balancing what different teams wanted, it was designing for multiple user journeys, each with different goals and needs, while keeping the overall experience consistent.

The decision I drove was to apply mixed qualitative research methods like stakeholders workshops, user surveys, and users interviews with end users and internal staff. I synthesized themes into a shared document so teams without a UX background could see the same evidence. That work surfaced a few key findings:

01 · Surveys & account data

What we wanted to know: how much of the subscription went unused, and how people prefer to learn.

How we checked: I reviewed how enterprise clients had set up their accounts, then ran two surveys with B2B and B2C learners.

What we found: 41% of top-tier features were never switched on. People learn by watching videos and reading docs, and barely touch in-app help. That way, the product connects people with training they already trust.

02 · Focus groups

What I wanted to know: which screen would people struggle with most?

How I checked: two focus groups on the prototype. I asked people to find an assessment for their role and interest, then read a skill-gap report.

What I found: the report was easy. The filter was not, people could not match their interests to an assessment. So we rebuilt it with simpler, standard controls and tested it again before engineering locked it in.

03 · Interviews & Workshops

What I wanted to know: Who was this product really for, were we designing for one audience, or for several types of users with different goals and needs.

How I checked: I interviewed enterprise tech managers (B2B), learners (B2C) and internal staff, then ran four workshops with Sales, Marketing, Curriculum, and Engineering.

What I found: the main finding was that we had to treat internal users as their own user journey. Giving those teams the proper internal tools would unlock significant operational cost savings. Managers wanted their team’s skills in one view, Sales needed that report to bring to customer meetings, and Curriculum needed to write and reuse questions, none of which a generic catalog could do. That is what finally changed the spreadsheet: internal users became first-class, not an afterthought.

3 user personas

The three user personas: enterprise technical managers, developers, and internal Red Hat users, started as assumptions. We used them to map out the journeys, then tested whether people's actual behavior backed them up.

Hypothesis · Managers

Need full visibility of team skills to spend budget on learning instead of unused services.

Hypothesis · Developers

Need assessments that match the work they actually do, not a generic certification path.

Hypothesis · Internal users

Need a report that helps a technical manager choose a learning subscription over more Red Hat services.

These were not finished truths. They were bets we took into flows, then into usability tests.

Each finding had to become a design decision, including where the primary action lives in each context.

Finding

Managers need a centralized view of team skills.

Design decision

A reporting dashboard as its own journey. Design

Finding

Learners cannot find relevant assessments in a static catalog.

Design decision

Filter-first information architecture on the landing page. Design

Finding

Internal teams are first-class users, and operational savings depend on them.

Design decision

A dedicated authoring space of their own, instead of bolting onto the learner UI. Design

Finding

The technical team built assessments slowly and copied questions by hand across assessments.

Design decision

A YAML editor and React Flow to speed authoring, with questions reused across assessments instead of duplicated, which cut maintenance work. Design

05 · The key insight

The real requirements came from users, not spreadsheets.

The turning point was treating the requirements spreadsheet as a set of assumptions to test, rather than a final specification. Research revealed three distinct user journeys: learners needed an easier way to find relevant assessments, managers needed a centralized view of team skills, and internal teams needed dedicated tools to create, reuse, and maintain assessment content efficiently. Business goals still mattered, but user evidence shaped how the product delivered them.

06 · Process

UXR → Prototyping: user flows & wireframes → High-fidelity screens applying the Red Hat Design System → User testing

My design process is iterative and user-centered. I start by conducting user research to understand the needs and pain points of the users. This helps me to define the problem and develop the user personas. I then ideate and brainstorm possible solutions, creating wireframes and prototypes to test with users and stakeholders. I iterate on the design based on user feedback until we’re satisfied that the solution is user-friendly and meets the needs of the target users.

UXR findings surfaced the core problems and the three personas driving it. From there, I mapped role-based user journeys for enterprise technical managers, developers, and internal Red Hat users.

We initially framed the product as primarily B2C learner experience, underestimating the internal users. As we worked through stakeholder workshops, we realized that Sales, Curriculum, and developers were also key users. This shifted our approach to a multi-role product, considering different goals, workflows, and pain points across each role.

Prototyping · user flowsThree role-based journeys, one system

Prototyping · Wireframes · Structure before visual design

During the wireframing phase, I focused on defining the information architecture, page structure, and key interactions across the role-based user journeys we had defined.

I worked closely with the technical team, using wireframes and prototypes to clarify requirements, understand constraints, and gather feedback. This helped us align on the experience and move into development with a clearer, shared understanding of the final experience.

High-fi · Red Hat Design System

At this critical stage, I meticulously incorporated Red Hat's design principles, visual elements and brand aesthetics into the user interface, aiming to maintain a cohesive and recognizable brand identity.

I created simple, intuitive UI components that followed Red Hat’s standards and helped build trust with users

Finding in action · 01 Sales. A report that names client skill gaps and also works as a sales presentation.

Finding in action · 03 Curriculum. The same question can appear in different assessments, so authoring and maintenance do not duplicate work.

Finding in action · engineering. YAML editor and React Flow so assessments could ship without a ticket pile.

User testingBefore engineering locked the filter

I ran usability sessions with two focus groups against the prototype: structured tasks plus open questions. The main challenge was not the report. It was the filter. People could not map their interests to an assessment without friction. We simplified the interaction and the layout before engineering locked it.

What worked especially well was the new skill-gap report experience. Close collaboration with engineering helped us to identify the technical constraints and validate the UX in the working context of the final product.

07 · Validation & outcome

Two test groups found the filter too hard. We fixed that before launch, then the business number moved.

The sessions in Process changed the filter before engineering locked it. What held after that was the business result: learning subscription sales and monthly visits on the live product.

08 · Reflection

We almost forgot the people who keep the product alive

We almost underbuilt the internal flow, the path Sales, Curriculum, and developers actually needed to keep the product alive. I fixed it by putting that journey on the same map as the learner, then teaching stakeholders how to read the research so they would fund it. Next time I would test the sales-deck report with Account teams before investing in catalog polish. The 15% sales lift and 10,000 monthly visits are what that correction bought. The cost was time: months spent convincing rooms that a spreadsheet of requirements was not the same thing as a user need.
