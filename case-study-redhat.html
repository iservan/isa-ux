03 · The problem

41% of features in the high-tier cloud subscription were never set up.

Red Hat enterprise clients were buying cloud solutions they could not fully use. Teams had no shared view of technical skill, So training was based on guesses, lost value was seen as “just the way it is”.

41% of features in the high-tier cloud subscription were never set up, the product was paid for and still invisible in daily work.
32% boost in cloud server utilization was possible with the right training.

Curriculum had already documented business requirements in a long spreadsheet. Those requirements described what the business wanted to ship, not what users needed to.

I led the UX research, collaborating cross-functionally with Sales, Marketing, and Curriculum teams. The real challenge wasn't just balancing different team priorities: Sales wanted conversion, Marketing wanted engagement, Curriculum wanted the content to be centered. The bigger issue was that none of those requirements actually reflected what users needed.

The decision I drove was to apply mix qualitative research methods like stakeholders workshops, user surveys, and users interviews to make sure what we were building actually matched what users needed, not just what was on the requirements spreadsheet. That work surfaced a few key findings:

  • End customers needed the ability to build custom assessments rather than one-size-fits-all templates.
  • Managers could not see team skill at a glance, so budget went to unused services instead of targeted learning.
  • Learners could not filter assessments by role, product, or interest, so they abandoned the path.
  • Red Hat technical team need it better developer tools, like a YAML editor and React Flow integration to speed up how they built assessments. They also needed the ability to reuse questions across different assessments, instead of duplicating them everywhere, which would cut down on maintenance work.

04 · Research & discovery

I talked to the people closest to the data, then treated personas as hypotheses.

2 surveys with B2B and B2C learners
4 Design Thinking workshops with stakeholders
Weekly engineering follow-ups
35 stakeholders across Sales, Marketing, Curriculum, Engineering

My first job was not to draw screens. It was to validate Curriculum’s spreadsheet against real work. I ran workshops with Marketing, Sales, and Engineering, interviewed end users and internal staff, and synthesized themes into a shared document so teams without a UX background could see the same evidence.

The three personas that followed, technical managers, developers, and internal Red Hat users, were working assumptions. We used them to structure journeys, then tested whether the behaviors held.

01 · Sales

Needed a report that named client skill gaps and doubled as a sales deck. Without that artifact, conversations stayed generic.

02 · Learners

Needed dynamic filtering by role, interest, and product. A static catalog made assessment selection slow and irrelevant.

03 · Curriculum & engineering

Needed a YAML editor, React Flow, and question reuse. Duplicate items were slowing authoring and breaking consistency.

04 · Alignment

Stakeholders could not interpret research on their own. Walking them through methods was how we got permission to change the requirements.

Hypothesis · Managers

Need full visibility of team skills to spend budget on learning instead of unused services.

Hypothesis · Developers

Need assessments that match the work they actually do, not a generic certification path.

Hypothesis · Internal users

Need a report that helps a technical manager choose a learning subscription over more Red Hat services.

These were not finished truths. They were bets we took into flows, then into usability tests.

05 · The key insight

Balancing Sales, Marketing, and Curriculum would still have failed if we never asked the users.

The pivot was to treat the spreadsheet as a hypothesis, then replace it with what research actually found: custom assessments for customers, centralized group reporting for corporate users, and technical tools so developers could maintain volume. Conversion, engagement, and content still mattered. They just could not be the spec.

06 · Process

UXR → Prototyping: user flows & wireframes → High-fidelity screens applying the Red Hat Design System → User testing

My design process is iterative and user-centered. I start by conducting user research to understand the needs and pain points of the users. This helps me to define the problem and develop the user personas. I then ideate and brainstorm possible solutions, creating wireframes and prototypes to test with users and stakeholders. I iterate on the design based on user feedback until We’re satisfied that the solution is user-friendly and meets the needs of the target users.

Prototyping · user flowsThree journeys, one system

UXR findings named the problem and the three personas. I created three user journeys based on their needs: technical managers, developers, and internal Red Hat users.

We almost treated this as a B2C learner product and underbuilt the internal path. As we mapped the flows, it became clear that Sales, Curriculum, and developers were also key users.

Prototyping · Wireframes · Structure before visual design

I worked closely with the technical team, using the wireframes and prototypes to clarify requirements.

High-fi · Red Hat Design System

At this critical stage, I meticulously incorporated Red Hat's design principles, visual elements and brand aesthetics into the user interface, aiming to maintain a cohesive and recognizable brand identity.

I created simple, intuitive UI components that followed Red Hat’s standards and helped build trust with users

Finding in action · 02 Learners. Support dynamic filtering so users can select skills for different roles. Assessments become relevant instead of a static catalog.

Finding in action · 01 Sales. A report that names client skill gaps and also works as a sales presentation.

Finding in action · 03 Curriculum. The same question can appear in different assessments, so authoring and maintenance do not duplicate work.

Finding in action · engineering. YAML editor and React Flow so assessments could ship without a ticket pile.

User testingBefore engineering locked the filter

I ran usability sessions with two focus groups against the prototype: structured tasks plus open questions. The main challenge was not the report. It was the filter. People could not map their interests to an assessment without friction. We simplified the interaction and the layout before engineering locked it.

What held up: the skill-gap report and the internal authoring path. Collaboration with engineering meant those pieces were already technically real, not slideware.

07 · Validation & outcome

Two test groups found the filter too hard. We fixed that before launch, then the business number moved.

The sessions in Process changed the filter before engineering locked it. What held after that was the business result: learning subscription sales and monthly visits on the live product.

08 · Reflection

We almost forgot the people who keep the product alive

We almost underbuilt the internal flow, the path Sales, Curriculum, and developers actually needed to keep the product alive. I fixed it by putting that journey on the same map as the learner, then teaching stakeholders how to read the research so they would fund it. Next time I would test the sales-deck report with Account teams before investing in catalog polish. The 15% sales lift and 10,000 monthly visits are what that correction bought. The cost was time: months spent convincing rooms that a spreadsheet of requirements was not the same thing as a user need.
