Collective Minds Radiology · A healthcare service design

Bringing radiologists together worldwide to solve rare cases through shared MRI's.

I joined as the first in-house designer and spent more than six months on research: 1:1 interviews, usability tests with focus groups, and observation of radiologists at their hospital workstations. The result was a service design, integrated into the day to day rutines of radiologists and clinical trial coordinators. A small hardware device, with a gateway sits beside the hospital PACS, prepares each MRI, and uploads it to the cloud, managed by a custom web and mobile app.

03 · The problem

Diagnosing rare cases required collaboration.
Sharing MRI scans was a struggle.

A radiologist read the MRI on a black diagnostic display wired to the hospital PACS. A coordinator at another trial site needed the same study. Between them sat a manual export: someone pulled files off the PACS and sent them. Patient identity travelled with the images, a series could arrive incomplete, and the person waiting could not open the scan until the last file landed.

The research moved the problem earlier in the path. The MRI had to be prepared inside the hospital, before anything went to the cloud. The online product could then start from a study that was already ready.

04 · Research & discovery

Six months of listening, then watching.

Research ran for more than six months, next to the build. I interviewed radiologists and clinical trial coordinators one to one, tested the posting flow with focus groups, and sat with radiologists at their workstations in the hospitals. I mapped what I saw as a service: who touches a study, where it sits, and what has to happen before it leaves the building.

01 · One-to-one interviews

What I wanted to know: who starts a case, who needs to see it, and what “done” means for each of them.

How I checked: 1:1 interviews with radiologists and trial coordinators, repeated across the six months.

What I found: radiologists present a case, suggest a diagnosis, reply, and search, with the images in reach. A coordinator needs the same study at another site, with identity already handled.

02 · Focus groups

What I wanted to know: where people lost the study they had just selected.

How I checked: recorded usability tests with focus groups on the posting prototype, the wizard against a single screen.

What I found: one task was split across three screens. People needed the study table, the image preview, date filters, and help together.

03 · Hospital workstations

What I wanted to know: how an MRI really leaves the building.

How I checked: I observed radiologists at the review workstation, and followed studies pushed from the PACS to the coordinators who received them.

What I found: radiologists review on black displays and asked for the same online. The handoff had to start inside the hospital, so we designed a small device on the PACS, Collective Minds Connect. It receives the MRI, prepares it, and uploads it to the cloud.

The radiologist job, written down early

The first personas were working documents from the interviews. The jobs were already specific. The empty fields were the questions for the next sessions: how often people would teach with a prepared study, and how a coordinator’s week runs once the MRI stops travelling as a file.

Hypothesis · Radiologist

Wants to teach and ask for a second read on the black field they already use, once the hospital has prepared the MRI.

Hypothesis · Trial coordinator

Wants the study at another site with identity handled before upload and the series still readable.

Hypothesis · Institution

Keeps the PACS as the source of truth. A device in the hospital prepares the MRI before the cloud receives it.

These were bets. Focus-group tests turned the posting bet into one screen. Workstation observation turned the integration bet into Connect.

Each finding became a decision in the service, from the device in the hospital to the screen a radiologist opens.

Finding

An MRI should be prepared inside the hospital, before it goes to the cloud, to protect patient identity.

Design decision

A small hardware gateway on the PACS that receives DICOM, pseudonymizes per channel, and uploads the prepared study. Connect

Finding

In the hospital, radiologists read scans on black diagnostic displays, and they asked for the same look in the product.

Design decision

A black interface for every review surface, so a scan reads the way it does at the workstation. Design

Finding

Radiologists discuss a case by suggesting a diagnosis and replying, and they still need the scan at full size while they do it.

Design decision

A case view with tags, confidence, replies, and similar cases, plus a full-screen review mode with a way back to the case. Case

Finding

A hospital or a trial works as a team, and the team needs one shared place to post and follow its cases.

Design decision

Group workspaces, so a team posts into a shared room. Design

05 · The key insight

Radiologists trust their workstation, so the service meets them there.

Every radiologist I observed worked inside one trusted setup: a black diagnostic display, the hospital PACS, and a study they knew was complete. A new tool earns trust when it feels like an extension of that setup. So the service begins at the workstation. Connect prepares the MRI inside the hospital and protects patient identity before anything is uploaded, and the online review looks and reads like the place radiologists already work.

06 · Process

Blueprint first, device second, screens last

I worked in the order the service needed. A blueprint of the whole path came first, then the device in the hospital, then the screens a radiologist uses. The design system grew alongside, so a growing team shared one black canvas and one coral action color.

ConnectThe device in the hospital

Collective Minds Connect is the back stage: a small hardware device that sits with the hospital’s own systems and prepares each MRI where it already lives. The hospital PACS pushes to it over standard DICOM communication. Below are the implementation details, taken from the Connect documentation published today.

Inflow
DICOM instances pushed from the PACS, or files dropped into designated folders.
Channels
Several channels run in parallel. Each has its own receiver, transformation, and destination.
Pseudonymization
It follows the NEMA 2024b standard, and SHA2-512/256 hashing keeps a study traceable without exposing the patient.
Transfer
The whole payload arrives, or the log records exactly what did not.
Network
Connect talks only to company-owned domains on specific ports, so the hospital firewall needs a minimal rule.
Runtime
Runs on Windows Server, LSB-compliant Linux, and macOS, and updates itself without human intervention.
Observability
Activity logs and health metrics show transfers and the health of the system.
Destination
The DICOM Study Service, which feeds Teaching and Research.

Once Connect has done its work, the radiologist meets the study on the posting screen. It is the first option, listed under the hospital that sent it. The page checks the patient ID and states that the review is anonymous. Uploading from a computer is the second option, for a case that never went through a connected PACS.

Post a caseThe front stage

This is the screen where a radiologist starts a case. They pick a study the hospital has already sent, or upload DICOM, PNG, JPG, or a screenshot. The study table and the previewer sit side by side, so selecting a row shows its key images before anything is posted. Once posted, the case opens for discussion with its key images, a suggested diagnosis, replies, and similar cases.

Design systemAtomic Design, built with the product

As the team grew from 3 to 27, I built the company design system from scratch on Atomic Design, together with the engineering lead. Small parts like the coral button, the tag, and the study row combine into the case card, the diagnosis block, and the image previewer. Web and mobile share them, and I coded them in Vue.js.

08 · Reflection

The best design decision happened outside the screen.

Six months with radiologists changed where I look for answers. Interviews told me what they needed to do. Watching them at the workstation showed me what they trusted: a black display and the PACS. The most useful decision followed from that, and it lives in the hospital as a small device that prepares the MRI before the cloud ever sees it.

It taught me to design the whole path first and the screens second. A service has a front stage and a back stage, and the back stage comes with constraints, such as what a hospital network will let through. Treating those constraints as design material kept the screens simple.

As the first designer, I also learned to build for the people who would come after me. The design system, made with the engineering lead, let a team that grew from 3 to 27 ship on one black field. The product is live today at collectiveminds.health.
