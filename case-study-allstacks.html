Product Studio · AI product management

Teams plan 4x faster and spend 32% less on AI tokens after Product Studio turned a rough idea into a build-ready spec.

Allstacks is a Series A startup building AI workflow tools for product and engineering. Product Studio is the 0-to-1 conversational layer that grounds those specs in the actual codebase, customer feedback, and delivery metrics, not a blank prompt.

Role
Led UX research, conceptualization, and UX/UI for Product Studio. I owned how the conversational flow earned trust.
Team

Tools

Timeline
6 months, 0→1 launch.
Platform
Desktop / web. Live: allstacks.com/product/product-studio

03 · The problem

Specs written by hand had no codebase, no customer voice, and no delivery history. Rework showed up after engineering had already started.

Project managers were burning time, and tokens, on vague requirements. The artifact looked like a spec. It did not contain the context engineers actually need: what the code already does, what customers asked for, what delivery history would block the plan.

1 day A customer CTO described planning a complete work tree in Claude as a full-day job, before Product Studio.
Too late Readiness gaps, security, architecture, QA, showed up in refinement, after the team was already in the work.

The failure was specific. A prompt-only AI tool still starts from a blank window. Feed it enough raw context and precision drops. Product Studio had to be the opposite: small, cited, and grounded in the Allstacks Context Graph, or it would be another expensive guess.

  • Hand-written specs lacked real context from the codebase, stakeholders, and customer calls.
  • Engineers inherited confusion. Costly rework showed up too late.
  • Generic LLM sessions were slow, unshared, and not persistently updated across PM, EM, and developers.

04 · Research & discovery

I sat with project managers, then treated the three personas as hypotheses against usage data.

PMs user interviews on how specs get written today
3 persona hypotheses, checked against analytics
15 people across product, engineering, marketing
Live prototypes in the browser, on real design-system components

I worked with project managers to map the current spec path, then designed an AI flow that had to feel fast, clear, and trustworthy. We named three personas, enterprise product roles, mid-size product leaders, and internal stakeholders, and compared those assumptions with actual usage. They were bets, not finished truths.

01 · Context on screen

Users needed to see connected tools, Jira, GitHub, Slack, before they would start a draft. Hidden data felt like guessing.

02 · Proof, not a score

A readiness number was not enough. Each finding had to cite the ticket, commit, or call behind it, or teams would not act.

03 · Idea to spec

PMs wanted to go from a rough idea to a structured plan without a week of research. “Easy button” was the job, not a chatbot toy.

04 · Different pressures

Enterprise roles needed compliance and analytics before an exec review. Startup leaders needed speed and fewer surprises. Same workflow, different proof.

Hypothesis · Enterprise PMs

Need a hub for compliance, reporting, and internal politics. Will not ship a spec they cannot defend.

Hypothesis · Startup product leaders

Need technical constraints and stakeholder feedback in the spec so they can pivot without rework.

Hypothesis · Stakeholders

Need to trust the plan before it goes to build. Constraints from engineering and design have to show up early.

We checked these against analytics after launch. The workflow was shared. The proof each role needed was not.

05 · The key insight

Every screen had to answer one question: can I trust this enough to act on it?

A conversational UI that writes faster is still a liability if the output cannot be traced. The product decision was to make evidence the spine: connected tools on the welcome screen, cited findings on the readiness report, sync to Jira only after the plan could survive a review. Speed and cheaper tokens follow from that, they are not the design.

06 · Process, shown not told

The conversation is the product. I designed it as a research loop, then tested it on the real design system.

I built and maintained a unified design system in Figma and Vue 3, tokens, components, production. Interactive prototypes used those same components so engineering saw the interaction, not a picture of it. That also showed where the system had to stretch.

07 · Validation & outcome

Customers measured planning in hours instead of a day. Token cost dropped. The design system had to catch up in public.

We validated in the browser with real components, not slideware. Some gaps were mixed: the prototypes exposed places the Vue 3 system was not ready, option boxes, readiness running states, and we extended the system instead of faking it in Figma. That slowed a few screens. It also meant engineering inherited working pieces.

On the live product, teams report faster plan creation and cheaper token use because the graph routes small, cited calls instead of stuffing a single LLM window.

4x faster plan creation
32% cheaper on tokens

08 · Reflection

I would have tested the evidence layer with PMs before polishing the chat.

The process broke down where I treated conversation as the hard part. It was not. The hard part was making a score believable. We found that late, in prototypes, when a readiness number without citations failed the “would I take this into a leadership review?” test. I fixed it by putting connected tools and cited findings on the same spine as the prompt. Next time I would prototype the proof panel first, even ugly, and only then dress the dialogue. The 4x planning speed and 32% token drop are what that correction bought. The cost was extra design-system work in the open, which was the right cost.
